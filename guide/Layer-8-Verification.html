Layer 8 · Verification

Silence is not health

An automated system fails quietly by default. It carries on running, reporting success, and producing nothing, and there is nobody sitting next to you to notice.

What the last layer left you with

Your system is fast, safe and ordered by a belief about what matters. Nothing has tested that belief. And nothing tells you whether the whole system is still doing its job, because a system with nothing to report and a system that has broken look identical from the outside.

What this layer builds

2 checks. One asks whether what you are doing actually works, counted from your own records. The other asks whether your system is doing what you told it to, and says so loudly when it is not.

Layer 8 · How it works

How it works

There are 2 checks here. One looks at whether your work is producing results, and one looks at whether the software is still doing its job.

Why you cannot use anyone else's numbers

A published figure about what works in sales has usually come from a company with something to sell, counting its own successes. That does not make it false, but it does mean it is measuring their customers, their market and their good days. It cannot tell you what works for you, and the only figures that can are your own.

Count all 4 boxes, not just the good one

Say you believe replying quickly gets more calls booked. The tempting move is to count the people you replied to quickly who then booked a call. That number will look impressive and it means nothing on its own.

You need all 4: you did it and it worked, you did it and it did not, you did not do it and it worked anyway, and you did not do it and it did not work. Counting only the successes is how people end up with a superstition they are certain about.

Even with all 4, this will not prove that what you did caused the result. You may simply have replied fastest to the people who were already keen. What it does do is stop you being certain of something your own numbers do not support.

Looking back at what came before a result

When something good happens, your system can now look backwards through your history and tell you what came before it. Sometimes the honest answer is that nothing you did was involved, which is worth knowing and slightly annoying.

The second check: what should be true

You write down, in plain English, a short list of statements that should be true of your system after it runs. Today's list should never be empty. Everyone on your do-not-contact list should still be on it. It should not have gone several runs without producing anything.

Then something checks that list against reality every time your system runs, and tells you when they disagree.

It reports. It never repairs

The check has no ability to fix anything, deliberately. Something that quietly corrects what it finds is one more program changing your records with nobody watching, and you would never know what it had done. It tells you. You decide.

Layer 8 · Before you start

Before you start on a Mac

Everything in this guide is typed into Terminal, the Mac's window for typing commands instead of clicking. Press Command and Space together, type Terminal, and press Return.

This layer needs Python and git, which you set up for Layer 1 (its page "Before you start on a Mac"), and the layers before this one. It installs no add-ons, so it works with either Python a Mac may have.

How to type the lines

Where this guide shows several lines in a black box, type them one at a time: type the first, press Return, wait for it to finish and give you a fresh line, then type the next. The installer asks you questions. Each offers an answer in [square brackets]; press Return to take it, or type your own and press Return.

Where your CRM is

Layer 1 made your CRM at ~/CRM: a folder called CRM in your home folder (the folder with your name and the house picture in Finder's sidebar). If you chose another folder in Layer 1, put your own folder wherever a line in this guide says ~/CRM. The installer finds your CRM by itself either way.

Apple Silicon or Intel (the 2 kinds of chip a Mac can have; the Apple menu, then About This Mac, shows yours): the steps are the same on both, and both were tested.

Tried only on test Macs. Every step in this guide and in the instructions file of the layer's download was tried on test Macs (Macs GitHub rents out by the minute to run a script, not a person's own Mac, and not yours), never on a real Mac, except 1: the box asking to install the developer tools, which cannot appear on a test Mac. The last pages list every line and what happened to it.

Build it

3 lines, typed into Terminal. The last one asks you a few questions so what you get is yours, then does the work.

Layer 8 · Finishing

Now use it

Break it on purpose. It is the only way to know the alarm works.

The tools this layer installed live in a folder called _engine inside your CRM. In Terminal, go there first, then run the watchdog (if your CRM is not at ~/CRM, put your own folder in the cd line). Most of it should pass.

cd ~/CRM/_engine
python3 watchdog.py

Now break something deliberately. Empty your list, or take someone off your do-not-contact list who should be there. Run it again.

python3 watchdog.py

It catches you and says exactly what is wrong.

That is worth doing once, because from here on you are trusting this check to tell you when something has gone wrong, and an alarm nobody has ever heard is not an alarm.

If you already had something like this

Your test: how would you currently find out that your system had stopped working? If the honest answer is that you would notice eventually, that is not a check, and it is the failure that costs the most because it costs you time you never knew you were losing.

What this layer leaves unsolved

Nothing new to build. But what you learn here changes the rules you wrote in Layer 2 and the sources you connected in Layer 4. Something you find out actually predicts a result earns a place in your system. A rule that keeps misfiring gets rewritten.

So you go back down the layers, change something near the bottom, and everything above it improves. That is not a sign the build is unfinished. A system that stops changing has stopped learning about the business it serves.

Next: Layer 2 · The rules

Back to the bottom with what you have learned. This is the last guide, but it is not the end of the build; it is the point where you start improving the system from what it tells you, rather than waiting for the next instalment.
